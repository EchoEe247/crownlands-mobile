# Game Vision

## One-sentence pitch

**Be the king inside a living 3D medieval realm, where everyone has a life of their own until the Crown chooses to intervene.**

## Player fantasy

The player does not work toward becoming king. The opening state is already royal authority.

The player should be able to:

- wake or begin the day inside the royal castle
- walk anywhere important in third person
- sit on the throne and hold court
- receive petitioners, nobles, officers, merchants, clergy, villagers, and foreign envoys
- issue direct orders to almost any person
- issue group orders to guards, servants, officials, military officers, or the army
- inspect barracks, kitchens, workshops, markets, walls, farms, villages, stables, and treasury activity
- travel through the realm
- negotiate alliances or rivalries
- respond to crimes, shortages, unrest, raids, border problems, and war
- personally join combat when desired without turning the game into a pure action game

The core experience is not "click the kingdom menu." It is **physically living as the ruler inside the kingdom**.

## World tone

The world should feel cinematic and readable, closer to a strong medieval film/game environment than a generic browser prototype.

Visual direction:

- grounded medieval fantasy / historical-inspired
- strong silhouettes
- believable castle organization
- guards, banners, gates, barracks, markets, farms, roads, villages, walls
- no intentionally cheap placeholder look in final content
- stylization is acceptable if coherent and visually strong
- good motion matters as much as static geometry

## The Crownlands

The player's castle is the strongest major castle in the local region.

It should eventually contain or connect to:

- throne room / royal hall
- royal apartments
- council chamber
- great hall
- kitchens and food stores
- treasury
- armory
- barracks
- guard quarters
- army quarters / garrison area
- training yard
- blacksmith / workshops
- stables
- inner courtyard
- main gate
- walls and towers
- market district
- servants' and workers' areas
- nearby village
- farms
- roads / forest / river or other surrounding terrain

The kingdom cannot feel like everything exists only for the player. The castle should function as a place people actually inhabit.

## Rival powers

A rival castle exists farther down the world.

It should not simply spawn enemies at the player's gate. It has its own:

- ruler
- nobles
- army
- economy
- villages
- patrols
- objectives
- political relationships
- strengths and weaknesses

There can be additional minor houses, villages, religious or trade factions, mercenaries, and neutral powers as the game expands.

## Diplomacy

Relationships can include:

- alliance
- defensive pact
- trade agreement
- non-aggression agreement
- tribute
- marriage/dynastic relationship
- rivalry
- border dispute
- vassalage
- betrayal
- coordinated military campaign
- war

Foreign actors should send emissaries and react to what the king does.

## The strongest castle does not mean no challenge

The player's castle starts as the strongest one, but that strength creates responsibilities.

Threats should come from:

- coalitions
- supply pressure
- betrayal
- multiple-front war
- internal discontent
- economic disruption
- assassination attempts / intrigue
- raids
- siege preparation
- succession/political crises later

The point is not to make the player weak. The point is to make power interesting.

## Royal authority

The king has unusually broad direct command authority.

Orders can target:

- one NPC
- a role ("find the steward")
- a unit ("second gate watch")
- a department ("kitchens")
- Royal Guard
- field army
- an officer
- a settlement
- the whole realm

Orders should become concrete world tasks whenever possible. An order to "double the gate watch" should cause actual guards to change shift/posting, not only increment a hidden stat.

## Consequences

Orders can succeed, fail, take time, consume resources, upset people, improve loyalty, create shortages, or change future behavior.

Royal authority is strong, but the simulation should still respond plausibly.

## No generic strategy-game fallback

Avoid solving every system with a full-screen menu.

Menus can summarize information, but the preferred interaction order is:

1. world interaction
2. speaking to the relevant person
3. issuing a contextual order
4. seeing the order happen
5. using dashboards only for information that genuinely benefits from abstraction
